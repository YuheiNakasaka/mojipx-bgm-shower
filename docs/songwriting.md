# Songwriting for BGM Shower

This guide is for people writing songs for BGM Shower.
It collects what makes Japanese pop of the Showa and early Heisei eras and game music of the 8- and 16-bit eras moving, and turns it into concrete moves for this site's song format.
Nobody can listen while writing a song here, so the last section is a checklist to review a song against before it ships.

## What the research says

### Game music

An essay on what makes game music good splits it into three axes ([sound_laboratory, note](https://note.com/sound_laboratory/n/na7f0821bc34b)):

| Axis | Meaning | What we take from it |
|---|---|---|
| Music on its own | The track works as music without the game, and connects to a genre (hip-hop, acid jazz, metal, folk). The surprise of "this, in a game?" is moving in itself | Write each song as a convincing piece of a real genre (city pop, techno pop, fusion, drum and bass, Celtic), not as generic "game music" |
| Creativity from constraints | Few voices and little memory led to arpeggios that imply chords and loops that stay memorable; chip timbres are now chosen on purpose for their nostalgia | Imply rich chords with arpeggios, make the loop seam part of the composition, treat the pulse and triangle voices as the charm |
| Music that works in the experience | The same melody changes meaning with the situation; a motif returns in a new context | Reuse short motifs across sections (and, sparingly, across songs) in new harmonies, tempos and instruments |

Composer-oriented guides add these techniques ([GameScoreCraft](https://gamescorecraft.com/video-game-music-theory-techniques/), [the "Mario cadence"](http://gamemusictheory.blogspot.com/2011/04/mario-cadence.html)):

- **Modes set the scene at once.** Dorian sounds minor but hopeful (adventure), Lydian floats (sky, dream), Phrygian is exotic and dark (desert, villain), Mixolydian is major with an edge (heroic), Aeolian is somber.
- **A leitmotif of 3–7 notes** with a distinctive rhythm survives re-harmonizing, re-voicing and transposing.
- **Ostinato and pedal tones** drive without demanding attention; a static bass under moving chords builds suspense.
- **Borrowed chords** from the parallel minor (♭VI, ♭VII, iv) make a plain major loop heroic or wistful. ♭VI–♭VII–I is the triumphant "Mario cadence"; it arrives without a dominant.
- **Open loop endings.** End the loop on V, a sus chord, or a melody resting on the 2nd or 5th, so the music leans into its own restart.
- **Timbre gives harmony its meaning.** The same four chords are a village on plucked sine and a dystopia on hard saws.

### Japanese pop progressions

([flat.io](https://blog.flat.io/jpop-anime-chord-progressions/), [MasterTheScore](https://www.masterthescore.com/article/anime-chord-progressions), [Motifkit J-pop](https://motifkit.com/jpop-chord-progressions/), [Motifkit city pop](https://motifkit.com/city-pop-bossa-nova-chord-progressions/), [UtaTen](https://utaten.com/live/chord-progression-emo/))

| Name | Degrees (C major) | Feeling, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Royal road (王道進行) | IVmaj7 – V7 – iii7 – vi (Fmaj7 G7 Em7 Am) | Bittersweet lift: the dominant sidesteps the tonic onto iii, which shares two notes with I but turns its third minor. The most common chorus progression in J-pop |
| Komuro (小室進行) | vi – IV – V – I (Am F G C) | Urgent, rising; suits driving choruses. Variants color IV with add9 and V with sus4 |
| Marusa (丸サ進行) | IVmaj7 – III7 – vi7 – v7 I7 (Fmaj7 E7 Am7 Gm7 C7) | Floating, late-night; never lands on I. III7 is the secondary dominant of vi |
| Canon | I – V – vi – iii – IV – I – IV – V | Verses; hands over smoothly to a royal-road chorus (they share I, V, vi) |
| City pop loop | Imaj7 – vi7 – ii7 – V7 | Jazz turnaround in a pop coat; every chord a seventh |
| Falling fifths | IV – iii – vi – ii – V – I | Keeps rolling forward without settling |
| Diatonic walk / with major VI | IV – V – vi, IV – V – VI | Calm and pastoral; the major VI adds an unexpected lift |
| Minor ii–V and substitutes | viiø7 – III7 – vi, #IVø7 in place of I | Thins the stability, makes it ache |
| Rising or falling bass (クリシェ) | I – I/VII – I/♭VII – IV/VI …; IV – IV/V – iii … | A bass moving by step gives drive (rising) or longing (falling) |
| Tonic and subdominant only | I – IV – I – IV with 9ths | No dominant pull: a floating, chill loop |

Color, not just function:

- Sevenths and ninths on almost every chord; maj7 on the tonic keeps "a trace of unresolved color even at rest".
- Altered dominants (7♭9, 7#9, 7♭5), tritone substitutes, and chromatic-mediant slides (Imaj7 → ♭IIImaj7) for city pop.
- sus4 → 3 resolutions and IV/V hybrids (F/G) at the end of a phrase.
- **The melody puts ninths, sevenths and sixths on strong beats**: a hallmark of modern Japanese melodies.
- **The last chorus lifted a half or whole step** for the climax.

### Producers' sounds

- **TK-style dance pop** ([Real Sound](https://realsound.jp/2014/05/post-499.html)): hooks come from rhythm first. A short loop of a bar or less repeats, its highest note is the accent and stays nearly the same pitch, and halving the loop length adds speed. A synth riff sits at the top of the song's hierarchy and the vocal line is built from the riff's rhythm. The low end stays simple (four-on-the-floor kick, bass on the root in straight eighths or sixteenths, offbeat hats) while the highs carry hooks; filler notes are either long or short, rarely in between; restraint ("subtraction") keeps every accent audible.
- **Early-nineties guitar-band pop** (from the repertoire): bright major keys, royal-road and IV–V–iii–vi choruses, an eight-beat rock kit and eighth-note chord strokes, verses that start on an upbeat, a chorus that opens on a long high note, sus4 → 3 at phrase ends, a guitar-solo-like lead in the interlude.
- **Techno pop** (from the repertoire): sixteenth-note sequencer basslines on root and octave, arpeggios that never stop, straight robotic timing, staccato square leads, pentatonic and Dorian melodies (minor key with a major IV), call-and-response between synths.
- **City pop** ([babyaud.io](https://babyaud.io/blog/what-is-city-pop) and the repertoire): extended chords, ii–V chains, slash chords, a syncopated slap-like bass with approach notes, sixteenth-note guitar or electric-piano cutting, string pads and brass stabs, melodies full of anticipations and ninths.
- **Showa ballads and kayo**: minor keys with harmonic-minor V7 (often 7♭9), the minor iv in major keys, a lead with wide vibrato, a string countermelody answering every phrase.
- **Eurobeat**: four-on-the-floor kick, open hats on the offbeats, an octave bass in eighths, a synth-brass riff as the song's identity.

## Song structure

A loop of 60–110 seconds is the target: long enough to tell a story, short enough to come round twice in a shuffle (loops longer than 100 s play once).

| Form | Use |
|---|---|
| intro – A – B – S – interlude – B – S – S↑ – outro (loop from A) | J-pop single. A = verse (A メロ), B = pre-chorus (B メロ), S = chorus (サビ), S↑ = chorus lifted a step |
| intro – A – A' – B – A'' (loop from A) | Game field or town theme: A states the motif, B contrasts (relative key, new mode, half time), A'' comes home varied |
| A – B – C – B' (loop from A) | Action stage: each section turns the screw; C is the breakdown or the bridge |
| intro – A – B – A – B – C (loop from A) | Ending theme or ballad: C is the emotional peak before the loop |

Section roles:

- **Intro**: the riff or the motif, often without drums (`drums: false`) or with only the pad. It plays once.
- **A (verse)**: middle-low register, talky rhythm, room to breathe, sparse band (`pad: false`, a lighter `drums` or `bass` pattern).
- **B (pre-chorus)**: rising sequences, harmony away from the tonic (IV, ii, iii, a secondary dominant), half time or a pushing `syncopate`, ends on V or sus4 to set up the chorus.
- **S (chorus)**: the highest notes, long tones, the royal road or Komuro loop, the `counter` line and `lead: 'alt'` for a brighter voice, full drums.
- **Interlude**: the intro riff again, or a lead "solo" on `alt`.
- **S↑ (finale)**: `transposed(chorus, 1 | 2, key)` lifts it, often after a one-bar pivot on the new key's V7sus4 → V7. An outro modulates back (a chromatic-mediant chord makes the step down smooth) so the loop can restart.

## Melody craft

- **Motif, then development.** State a 2–4-beat idea, repeat it, sequence it a step up or down, and change its ending. Repetition is what makes a tune hummable.
- **Contour by section.** A low and conversational, B climbing by sequence, S starting high or leaping up, peaking near the end of its second half.
- **Rhythm hooks.** Give each section a rhythmic cell (dotted, syncopated, a run of sixteenths, a pickup) and keep it; vary the pitches.
- **Anticipation (食い).** Strike the next bar's note on the last eighth of the bar and tie it over. It makes a chorus surge. The lint hears such a note against the chord it anticipates.
- **Long notes on color.** Hold the 9th, the maj7 or the 6th on a downbeat, then resolve or leap.
- **Breathe.** End phrases with rests; a melody that never stops is wallpaper.
- **Call and response.** Let the counter line answer in the melody's rests, or move in thirds and sixths under the chorus.
- **Leaps and steps.** A leap up (a 6th or an octave) followed by stepwise descent is the classic emotional gesture.
- **Mode for color.** Raised 4th for dreams (Lydian), raised 6th in minor for hope (Dorian), lowered 7th in major for heroic or bluesy (Mixolydian), lowered 6th borrowed for longing.
- **Loop seam.** The last bar should lead into the first: end on V or sus, keep the last note within an octave of the first.

## The song format

```ts
import { type SongDef, transposed } from '../../domain/music/song.ts';

const chorus = { chords: 'Abmaj7 | Bb7 | Gm7 | Cm7', melody: '…', counter: '…', lead: 'alt' as const };

export const example: SongDef = {
  id: 'example', title: 'Example', style: 'city-groove', mood: { valence: 0.3, energy: 0.2 }, key: 'Eb', bpm: 108,
  sections: {
    intro: { chords: '…', melody: '…', drums: false },
    a: { chords: '…', melody: '…', pad: false, bass: 'half' },
    s: chorus,
    up: transposed(chorus, 1, 'E'),
  },
  form: ['intro', 'a', 's', 'up'],
  loopFrom: 1,
};
```

`src/content/songs/tail-lights.ts` is a complete example using every feature.

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `chords` | One or two chords per bar; two chords split the bar (2+2 beats in 4/4, 2+1 in 3/4) |
| `melody`, `counter` | mojipx tokens: `C5`, `F#4`, `Bb3` (flats are fine), `-` hold, `.` rest, `E4+G4` a dyad; one token per eighth (`steps: 2`, default) or sixteenth (`steps: 4`) |
| `bass`, `harmony`, `drums` | Pattern ids from `styles.ts`, or `false` to silence; per section, or song-wide in `parts` |
| `pad` | The style's sustained chord pad (on by default where the style has one) |
| `lead: 'alt'` | Plays the melody on the style's second lead voice (only styles with `alt`) |
| `key` | The section's own key, for modulation; `transposed()` sets it |
| `meter: 3` | Waltzes, and jigs (six eighths per bar feel like 6/8) |

Chord qualities: `''`, `m`, `dim`, `aug`, `sus2`, `sus4`, `7`, `maj7`, `m7`, `m7b5`, `dim7`, `6`, `m6`, `add9`, `madd9`, `9`, `maj9`, `m9`, `mM7`, `7sus4`, `9sus4`, `7b9`, `7#9`, `7b5`, `aug7`, `13`, `m11`, `maj7#11`, `69`; slash bass with `/` (`F/G`, `C/E`).

Patterns written in sixteenths (`funk`, `roll`, `sequence`, `gate`, `arp16`, `sparkle`, `cut`, `euro`, `city`, `break`) make the whole song play in sixteenths; melodies written in eighths are stretched automatically.

### What the musical checks allow

Every song must pass `lintSong` (see `docs/design.md`). In practice:

- Notes belong to the section's key or to the chord under them. Chromatic notes are fine when they are chord tones (E7's G# in C major).
- A non-chord tone of a beat or more must not sit a semitone above a chord tone (the 4th over a major chord, the ♭6 over a minor chord, the ♭9 over anything but a 7♭9 chord).
- A non-chord tone of two beats or more on beat 1 or 3 must be a tension: the 9th, the 6th (13th over dominants), the 11th over minor chords, the maj7 over a major triad.
- No leap beyond an octave, within the line or from the loop's end back to its start. Rests do not reset this: a counter line coming back after a long rest must start within an octave of its last note.
- Writing the color into the chord symbol widens what the melody may hold: over `6` or `69` the 6th is a chord tone, over `maj9` the 9th, over `13` the 13th.
- Melody and counter must not sit a semitone (or a minor 9th) apart for a beat or more.
- Two songs must not share 10 consecutive melodic intervals.

The library tests also hold every song to a minimum shape: a loop of 60–150 seconds with at least three sections, at least two textures (different band switches, counter or lead voice) and a counter line, and at least a third of its chords carrying a seventh, an extension, a suspension or a slash bass.

## Review checklist

Read the song back as a listener would, section by section, and answer each question honestly.

1. **Genre**: would a fan of the genre recognize it from the band alone (patterns, tempo, voices)?
2. **Hook**: can you sing the chorus after reading it once? Is there one rhythmic cell it keeps returning to?
3. **Contour**: is the chorus the highest and most sustained part? Does B climb into it?
4. **Color**: do downbeats carry 9ths, maj7s or 6ths somewhere? Is there at least one borrowed or secondary chord, a slash chord or a moving bass where it hurts most?
5. **Arc**: do sections differ in density (pad, drums, counter, alt lead), so the chorus opens up?
6. **Seam**: does the end of the loop lead into its start without a jolt?
7. **Breath**: are there rests at phrase ends? Does the counter line answer rather than clutter?
8. **Originality**: is the melody your own, not a half-remembered tune? Titles must be plain English and original.
